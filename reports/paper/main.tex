\begin{abstract}
\noindent Security operations centres (SOCs) can investigate only a limited number of alerts per analyst and per day, yet anomaly detectors for authentication logs are usually compared by threshold-free ranking metrics such as the area under the ROC curve. We present \AuthBench{}, an open benchmark that evaluates detectors under a strictly temporal protocol whose absence of leakage is enforced by code, and that reports, next to the usual metrics, campaign-level recall under a fixed daily alert budget and the exact daily budget at which a detector first detects any campaign. On the authentication log of the Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) comprehensive cyber-security events data (training on days 0--7, validation on days 8--10 and testing on days 11--13, with two days of history before each partition; \WideTestEvents{} test events containing \WideTestPositives{} red-team events grouped into \WideTestCampaigns{} campaigns), none of seven detectors (two floors, a rule engine, two statistical scores and two classical outlier detectors) detects a single campaign at 10 to 500 alerts per day, whatever the order of tied scores. The daily budget needed for a first detection ranges from \WideRulesFirstDetection{} alerts for the rule engine to \WideIForestFirstDetection{} for Isolation Forest, and two detectors with a ROC-AUC above 0.87 need more alerts than a random score. Over the five non-trivial detectors, the ordering induced by ROC-AUC does not track the operational ordering (Spearman $\rho = \WideSpearmanRocAuc$), whereas the ordering induced by the area under the precision-recall curve tracks it more closely ($\rho = \WideSpearmanAucPr$); with five detectors these coefficients are descriptive. Three qualifications bound the result and are measured rather than assumed: \WideTestTopHostShare{} of the red-team test events originate from a single host, so the campaigns are not independent intrusions; the rule engine is calibrated on validation labels whereas the other detectors use none; and no deep or graph-based detector has yet been evaluated under the protocol. A synthetic control shows how a rule engine can reach perfect recall on data whose generator shares its decision predicate, and detect nothing within the operational range against a red team that did not.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

An anomaly detector deployed in an enterprise network is consumed through an alert queue. Each day, analysts investigate the highest-ranked events until their capacity is exhausted, and the detector is useful only if an attack appears within that capacity. Interviews with SOC practitioners describe alert streams dominated by false positives \parencite{alahmadi2022}, and automated triage has been proposed to combat the resulting alert fatigue \parencite{hassan2019nodoze}. The base-rate argument of \textcite{axelsson2000} explains why: when attacks are rare, even a small false-positive rate produces an alert stream dominated by false alarms.

Authentication logs are an extreme instance of this regime. In the LANL data used here, the positive rate of the test partition is $\WideTestRate$. Detectors for such data are nevertheless commonly summarised by threshold-free metrics, most often the area under the ROC curve (ROC-AUC), which aggregates the ranking over every operating point, including those no SOC could staff. The precision-recall curve is known to be more informative than the ROC curve under class imbalance \parencite{davis2006,saito2015}, and evaluation pitfalls such as temporal snooping and inappropriate performance measures are documented across security machine learning \parencite{arp2022,pendlebury2019,sommer2010}. What remains less documented is the measurement itself: on real attack data, how far does the ordering of detectors by an aggregate metric diverge from their ordering at a budget an analyst can work through?

This paper makes four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{An evaluation protocol for budgeted detection} (\cref{sec:evaluation}). Events are ranked within each day; a campaign is detected at budget $k$ when one of its events is among the $k$ highest-ranked events of its day. We show that campaign recall at every budget is determined by one number per campaign, its detection budget, which yields an exact curve and the budget of first detection in a single pass. Ties are broken by a stated, label-free order and bracketed by their best and worst cases, and uncertainty is estimated by a paired, campaign-level bootstrap that reports when a $p$-value is only a bound.
\item \textbf{A leak-free benchmark whose temporal discipline is enforced by code} (\cref{sec:protocol}). A guard raises an error on any overlap between partitions, an architecture test forbids model and feature code from importing the test partition, windowed features are computed by causal primitives, and every fitted quantity is fitted on the training partition only.
\item \textbf{Measurements on the LANL red-team data} (\cref{sec:results}) for a rule engine, statistical scores and classical outlier detectors, including the relation between the threshold-free orderings and the operational ordering.
\item \textbf{Diagnostics that bound the claims} (\cref{sec:circularity,sec:threats}): the concentration of the red-team events on one source host, the transductive behaviour of two detectors, a cold-start encoding bias, and a control for circularity between a data generator and a rule.
\end{enumerate}
The benchmark proposes no new detector. Its claims concern evaluation, and their scope is stated in \cref{sec:scope}.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Evaluation pitfalls in security machine learning.}
\textcite{axelsson2000} established the base-rate fallacy for intrusion detection, and \textcite{sommer2010} argued that anomaly detection differs from the closed-world classification settings in which machine learning usually succeeds. \textcite{pendlebury2019} showed that spatial and temporal experimental bias inflates malware classification results. \textcite{arp2022} identified ten recurring pitfalls, among them data snooping, inappropriate performance measures and the base-rate fallacy, and \textcite{apruzzese2023sok} assessed the practical value of machine learning for network intrusion detection. Leakage in data mining more generally is formalised by \textcite{kaufman2012leakage}. \AuthBench{} turns several of these recommendations into mechanical checks and applies them to authentication data.

\paragraph{Metrics under class imbalance.}
ROC-AUC is invariant to the class ratio, whereas precision is not; \textcite{davis2006} related the two curves, and \textcite{saito2015} showed empirically that the precision-recall plot is the more informative one on imbalanced data. Neither curve, however, encodes a capacity constraint per unit of time, which is what an alert budget imposes.

\paragraph{Detection on the LANL data.}
The LANL comprehensive cyber-security events data \parencite{kent2015dataset,kent2016chapter} pairs 58 days of enterprise authentication events with labelled red-team activity, and its authentication graph has been characterised by \textcite{kent2015authgraphs}. \textcite{turcotte2016} model users relative to peer groups by Poisson factorisation. \textcite{tuor2018} apply recurrent language models to log lines; their operational metric is the average percentile of malicious events in a daily ranking, not average precision. \textcite{brown2018} add attention mechanisms to that family. \textcite{bowman2020} learn node embeddings on the authentication graph, training on the 40 days without reported malicious activity and testing on the 18 days with it; their best detector reports 635 true positives against 107{,}960 false positives. \textcite{king2022euler} cast lateral-movement detection as temporal link prediction (Euler) and train on the snapshots that precede the first anomalous edge. \textcite{xu2024argus} (Argus) and \textcite{paudel2022pikachu} (Pikachu) propose further graph-based detectors, and \textcite{gokstorp2024} apply transformer sequence models to the same logs.

\paragraph{Budgeted evaluation and recent re-evaluations.}
Budget-based metrics have been used on LANL before, and \AuthBench{} builds on them rather than introducing the idea. \textcite{garchery2020adsage} report cumulative recall at daily budgets of investigated user-days and find that a rule flagging previously unseen source computers performs on par with their graph-based detector. \textcite{larroche2021decades} report detection rates for a fixed daily budget of investigated events alongside a truncated ROC area. \textcite{larroche2025ultralmd} report, for the same detectors, ROC-AUC, average precision and the recall obtained when the top $B$ edges of each hourly window are investigated; in one of their configurations, the detector with the higher ROC-AUC has the lower budgeted recall. Two 2026 studies re-evaluate published detectors under harmonised conditions. \textcite{larroche2026fair} harmonise the preprocessing and labelling of LANL and OpTC, show that published average precision values do not survive the harmonisation (for Argus on LANL, 32.27 as originally reported against $0.09 \pm 0.04$ re-evaluated, in their Table~5), and note that almost all LANL red-team authentications come from one source host. \textcite{zhao2026gids} decompose graph-based intrusion detectors into interchangeable stages and find that an encoder-free control ranks first by average precision on two of four datasets.

\paragraph{Positioning.}
\AuthBench{} differs from these works in its unit and its question. It scores individual authentication events, ranks them within each day, and evaluates at the level of red-team campaigns; it compares rules, statistical scores and classical outlier detectors under one protocol; and it asks how the orderings induced by threshold-free metrics relate to the ordering at a fixed daily budget. Its first-detection budget generalises the budgeted recall of the works above from a set of budgets to the exact budget at which each campaign is first caught.

\section{Data, labels and campaigns}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Source}
We use the authentication log of the LANL comprehensive cyber-security events data \parencite{kent2015dataset}: 1{,}051{,}430{,}459 events over 58 days from one enterprise Windows Active Directory environment, with one-second timestamps starting at an arbitrary epoch. Each event records a source and destination user, a source and destination computer, an authentication type, a logon type, an orientation and an outcome. The file is verified against a recorded SHA-256 digest before use. The authentication type is missing for about 55\% of events and the logon type for about 14\%; missingness is encoded as a dedicated indicator rather than imputed. Machine accounts, whose names end in \texttt{\$}, are flagged and kept. Failed authentications appear only for users who succeed at least once in the data, so no conclusion is drawn about attacks on non-existent accounts.

\subsection{Labels}
The red-team file contains 749 lines, of which 34 are exact duplicates, leaving 715 distinct red-team events; the figure of 737 found in secondary sources is not reproduced by the file. Authentication events are labelled malicious by an exact join on the four red-team fields: time, user (with domain), source computer and destination computer. A looser join, for example on time alone, would inflate the number of positives. Red-team activity spans days 1 to 29; the remaining 29 days contain no labelled positive.

\subsection{Campaigns}
Red-team events are not independent observations. We group them into campaigns per user (with domain): two consecutive red-team events of the same user belong to the same campaign when they are separated by at most $g$ hours, with $g = 24$. No canonical grouping exists in the literature, and the threshold is a published parameter whose sensitivity analysis is planned (\cref{sec:threats}).

\subsection{Concentration of the red-team activity}
\label{sec:concentration}
The campaign is the resampling unit of the bootstrap (\cref{sec:uncertainty}), so the independence of campaigns matters. It is limited. In the test partition, \WideTestTopHostPositives{} of the \WideTestPositives{} red-team events (\WideTestTopHostShare{}) and \WideTestTopHostCampaigns{} of the \WideTestCampaigns{} campaigns originate from the same source computer, \TopHost{}; the shares are \WideValTopHostShare{} in validation and \WideTrainTopHostShare{} in training. This is consistent with the observation of \textcite{larroche2026fair} that the labelled LANL authentications belong to a single simulated intrusion. Our campaigns are therefore better read as the accounts and phases of one operation than as independent attacks, and the confidence intervals of \cref{sec:results} quantify variability over those accounts and phases, not over intrusions.

Three constraints determine this choice. Every partition must contain campaigns, otherwise ranking metrics are undefined in validation or test; temporal order must hold; and red-team activity stops at day 29. A contiguous split over the full 58 days in the proportions often used for this data (training on days 0--29, validation on 30--39, testing on 40--57) would place every red-team event in training. A unit test rejects any split that leaves a partition without campaigns. Day 11 contains no red-team event, so the test positives fall on days 12 and 13, five and six days after the last training day. The red-team activity of days 14 to 29 is not used in this evaluation.

\subsection{Leakage control}
Three mechanisms enforce the protocol rather than document it. A guard raises an error unless the last training timestamp precedes the first validation timestamp and the last validation timestamp precedes the first test timestamp. An architecture test fails if any module under \texttt{models/} or \texttt{features/} imports the only function that loads the test partition. Windowed features are computed by causal primitives: rolling aggregates use the left-closed window $[t-w, t)$, which excludes the current event, and statistics that a running sum cannot express use a self-join restricted to strictly earlier events of the same entity; a unit test builds events whose feature values would differ if the future were visible.

\subsection{Fitted quantities}
Two feature transformations are fitted: the night window, located at the empirical activity trough because timestamps carry no time zone, and the relative frequencies used to encode categorical fields. Both are fitted on the training partition only and applied unchanged to validation and test. Fitting them per partition would let an event's encoding depend on later events and would give one category different values in different partitions, without raising any error.

\subsection{Features}
\label{sec:features}
Detectors that consume a feature vector receive 21 columns from four families, listed in \cref{app:features}: event-level indicators and training-fitted category frequencies (F1); per-user event counts and failure ratios over the preceding hour and day (F2); novelty and rarity of the (user, destination computer) pair (F3); and the time of day on the unit circle with the deviation of an event from its user's running circular mean (F4). Novelty is defined relative to the events the partition reads, that is, the partition and its warm-up: a pair is \emph{new} if it has not occurred earlier in that window, not if it has never occurred in the data.

\paragraph{Exclusion of ECOD.} ECOD recomputes its empirical distributions from the matrix it scores, so the score of an event depends on the events scored alongside it (on the synthetic sample, the same event scores 10.64 alone and 10.81 among 400 others), and its scoring of the \WideTestEvents{}-event test partition exceeded the memory of the machine used. HBOS represents the histogram family.

\paragraph{Transductive normalisation in M1.} M1 rank-normalises each rule within the partition being scored, so it is also transductive: the score of a test event depends on the distribution of the other test events, although no label is involved. \section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Operational register}
Let $D$ be the test days, $E_d$ the events of day $d$, $P \subseteq \bigcup_d E_d$ the red-team events and $\mathcal{C}$ the campaigns, each campaign $c$ being a set $P_c \subseteq P$. A detector assigns a score $s(e)$ to every event. Within each day, events are ordered by decreasing score; tied scores are ordered by arrival, that is, by time and then by event identifier, an order that is total and independent of labels. Let $r(e) \in \{1, \dots, |E_d|\}$ be the resulting rank of $e$ in its day. The alert set at daily budget $k$ is
\begin{equation}
A(k) = \{\, e : r(e) \le k \,\},
\end{equation}
and event recall and campaign recall at budget $k$ are
\begin{equation}
\Rev(k) = \frac{|A(k) \cap P|}{|P|}, \qquad
\Rcamp(k) = \frac{|\{\, c \in \mathcal{C} : P_c \cap A(k) \neq \emptyset \,\}|}{|\mathcal{C}|}.
\end{equation}
We report $k \in \{10, 50, 100, 500\}$.

\paragraph{Detection budget.} Define the detection budget of a campaign as $b(c) = \min_{e \in P_c} r(e)$. A campaign is detected at budget $k$ exactly when $b(c) \le k$, hence
\begin{equation}
\Rcamp(k) = \frac{|\{\, c : b(c) \le k \,\}|}{|\mathcal{C}|} \quad \text{for every } k,
\end{equation}
so the whole campaign-recall curve is the empirical distribution function of $\{b(c)\}_{c \in \mathcal{C}}$, computable in one pass. We report the \emph{budget of first detection} $b^{*} = \min_{c} b(c)$, the smallest daily budget at which the detector detects anything. Unlike recall at a fixed budget, $b^{*}$ does not saturate at zero, and it is always finite when the partition contains a campaign. As a minimum, it is determined by a single campaign and should be read as an extreme of the curve, not as a summary of it.

\paragraph{Ties.} For a detector with coarse scores, which events enter $A(k)$ can depend on the tie order rather than on the detector. We therefore also report $\Rcamp(k)$ under the least favourable order (benign events first within each tied group), which is a lower bound, and under a favourable order (malicious events first, one per campaign before any second), and the number of tied events competing for the last places of each budget. A bracket of zero width certifies that the tie order decided nothing.

\paragraph{Time to detection.} For each detected campaign and budget, the delay between its first event and its first alert is recorded; campaigns never detected are counted separately and never enter the delays as zero.

\paragraph{Threshold-free metric.} The area under the precision-recall curve (AUC-PR) is estimated by average precision over the whole test partition. It is threshold-free, but, unlike ROC-AUC, it depends on the positive rate \parencite{davis2006,saito2015}. We use it as the threshold-free metric of reference and attach confidence intervals to it.

\subsection{Literature register}
ROC-AUC, precision at $k$ over the whole partition (not per day) and recall at a fixed false-positive rate of $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-5}$ are reported so that results can be placed next to published ones. They are not used to rank detectors, and the pipeline attaches a warning to every ROC-AUC it emits.

\subsection{Uncertainty}
\label{sec:uncertainty}
Events of one campaign are dependent, so resampling events independently would understate the variance. We use a block bootstrap \parencite{efron1993,field2007} whose blocks are the campaigns and the individual benign events: each resample draws, with replacement, as many blocks as there are, and every detector is evaluated on the same resample. Resamples without any positive, on which ranking metrics are undefined, are discarded and redrawn. With $R = 2000$ resamples, the 95\% interval of each detector is the percentile interval of its AUC-PR. Because all detectors share the resamples, the difference $\Delta^{*}$ between two detectors is paired. Its two-sided $p$-value is
\begin{equation}
p = \min\!\left(1, \frac{2\,(t+1)}{R+1}\right), \qquad t = \min\bigl(\#\{\Delta^{*} \le 0\}, \#\{\Delta^{*} \ge 0\}\bigr),
\end{equation}
and the family of 21 pairwise comparisons is corrected by the procedure of \textcite{holm1979}. When $t = 0$, no resample placed the difference on the other side of zero and $p$ equals its floor $2/(R+1)$; such $p$-values are reported as upper bounds. The number of resamples is chosen so that the floor, multiplied by the number of comparisons, stays below the significance level; with fewer than 1{,}119 resamples, Holm's procedure could not declare any of the 21 differences significant. No significance verdict is issued when fewer than two campaigns are present, because resampling a single campaign cannot vary the campaign composition.

\subsection{No detection within the operational range}
No detector detects any of the \WideTestCampaigns{} campaigns at 10, 50, 100 or 500 alerts per day, and the tie brackets show that no ordering of tied scores would change this. The result holds for both floors, for the rule engine, and for the four statistical and classical detectors.

\subsection{How far each detector is from a detection}
The budget of first detection separates detectors that the recall columns cannot. The rule engine first detects a campaign at \WideRulesFirstDetection{} alerts per day, PCA at \WidePcaFirstDetection{} and pair rarity at \WidePairRarityFirstDetection{}. HBOS needs \WideHbosFirstDetection{} and Isolation Forest \WideIForestFirstDetection{}, both more than the random floor (\WideRandomFirstDetection{}). These two detectors have a ROC-AUC of \WideHbosRocAuc{} and \WideIForestRocAuc{} respectively, values that would usually be read as good.

\subsection{Threshold-free orderings and the operational ordering}
Over the five non-trivial detectors, we compare the ordering induced by each threshold-free metric with the ordering by budget of first detection. For ROC-AUC, the Spearman coefficient is $\rho = \WideSpearmanRocAuc$ (Kendall $\tau = \WideKendallRocAuc$): the ROC-AUC ordering carries no information about the operational one. The two ends of the ordering are inverted: pair rarity has the highest ROC-AUC (\WidePairRarityRocAuc{}) and the third budget of first detection, whereas the rule engine has the lowest ROC-AUC (\WideRulesRocAuc{}) and the first. For AUC-PR, $\rho = \WideSpearmanAucPr$ ($\tau = \WideKendallAucPr$), in line with the sensitivity of precision to the positive rate. With five detectors, a single transposition changes these coefficients substantially, so we read them as a description of this catalogue, not as an estimate for detectors in general. The finding we retain is the narrower one: a ROC-AUC between 0.87 and 0.94 was compatible here with detectors whose first detection required between \WidePairRarityFirstDetection{} and \WideIForestFirstDetection{} alerts per day.

\subsection{Significance of the AUC-PR differences}
Of the \WidePairsTotal{} pairwise differences in AUC-PR, \WidePairsSignificant{} are significant after Holm's correction, and all \WidePairsAtFloor{} are at the resolution floor, with an adjusted $p \le \WideHolmFloor$ against a level of 0.05 (\cref{app:pairwise}). The three non-significant differences are random against always-fail, PCA against HBOS, and pair rarity against the rule engine. The statement that pair rarity and the rule engine differ in AUC-PR is therefore not supported, although they differ in ROC-AUC by almost 0.4. These verdicts concern AUC-PR only; no detector detects anything at the operational budgets, so no significance test of operational recall is meaningful on this run.

\subsection{Effect of history: the single-day configuration}
\label{sec:single-day}
An earlier configuration, constrained by an 8\,GB machine, used one day per partition (training on day 5, validation on day 8, testing on day 12) without warm-up (\cref{app:single-day}). Its test day is the first of the two test days with red-team activity in the evaluated configuration. Two observations follow. ROC-AUC values are stable between the configurations (for example \OnedayPairRarityRocAuc{} and \WidePairRarityRocAuc{} for pair rarity). The only non-zero operational result of the single-day configuration, the rule engine detecting 2 of 39 campaigns at 500 alerts per day (\OnedayRulesRecallAtFiveHundred{}), disappears in the evaluated one, where its first detection requires \WideRulesFirstDetection{} alerts. The test window and the warm-up changed together between the two runs, so this change cannot be attributed to either. A plausible mechanism is that, without warm-up, novelty features flag too many pairs as new (on the synthetic sample, two warm-up days halve the number of pairs flagged as new), and a rule built on novelty then ranks more red-team events highly; testing it requires a run that varies one factor at a time.

\section{A control for circularity}
\label{sec:circularity}

Before the LANL run, the protocol was exercised on a synthetic sample whose generator emits each campaign as a chain: a user authenticates from $A$ to $B$, then from $B$ to $C$. On that sample, the rule engine detects every test campaign at a budget of 10 alerts per day. A decomposition by rule (\cref{tab:rules}) explains why. Rule R7 tests whether the previous destination of a user equals the current source within 30 minutes, which is the generator's own predicate; it separates malicious from benign events by a factor of \DemoRSevenRatio{} and receives a calibrated weight of \DemoRSevenWeight{}. The result is a property of a sample written by the author of the rules, and a campaign-level bootstrap reports it with correct intervals all the same. Against the LANL red team, which did not know the rules, the same engine detects no campaign within the operational range of the evaluated configuration. A synthetic benchmark cannot distinguish a good heuristic from a heuristic informed by the generator; an independent adversary can.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\paragraph{Dependence of the campaigns.} The positives of all three partitions come predominantly from one host (\cref{sec:concentration}), and the labelled LANL activity is a single intrusion \parencite{larroche2026fair}. The bootstrap treats campaigns as independent units; its intervals describe variability across the accounts and phases of one operation, and the effective number of independent intrusions is far smaller than \WideTestCampaigns{}. Generalisation to other intrusions is not established by this run.

\paragraph{Unequal access to labels.} M1 calibrates its weights on validation labels; no other detector uses labels or validation data, and their hyperparameters are defaults. This favours M1. Equal tuning budgets for all detectors, selected on validation, are planned.

\paragraph{Transductive detectors.} M1 normalises rule scores by rank within the scored partition, and ECOD recomputes its distributions from the scored matrix. Neither uses a test label, but both scores depend on the test distribution. Fitting the normalisation on training data would remove this property from M1 and is planned with the next run.

\paragraph{Cold start encoded as normal.} For the first event of a user in the window, the deviation from the user's typical hour is set to zero, the most typical value, and the failure ratios are zero. On the synthetic test split, 350 events are first events of their user, and two of the eleven malicious test events are among them. This biases detectors that rely on these features towards ranking first appearances as normal, in the direction of the reported zeros.

\paragraph{Definition of novelty and rarity.} Pair novelty and pair rarity are computed relative to the window the partition reads, not to the whole history; and because the denominator of the rarity score grows within the window, a pair with a fixed number of earlier occurrences receives a slightly higher rarity later in the window.

\paragraph{Fitting sample, exclusions and configuration.} Distribution-estimating detectors are fitted on \num{5000000} of the \WideTrainEvents{} training events. ECOD is excluded. The single-day and evaluated configurations differ in two factors at once (\cref{sec:single-day}). Day boundaries are defined on the dataset's arbitrary epoch, not on local time.

\paragraph{Campaign definition.} Campaign recall depends on the grouping threshold $g$; a sensitivity analysis over several values of $g$ is planned.

\paragraph{External validity.} One enterprise network, observed in 2015, before the widespread adoption of multi-factor and token-based authentication. Labels are a partial ground truth: an unlabelled event may be an undetected compromise. The red team did not attempt to evade these detectors, so the results do not describe an adaptive adversary.

\section{Scope of the claims}
\label{sec:scope}
The results do not show that anomaly detection on authentication logs cannot work. They show that, for the seven detectors and the protocol above, on the test partition of one network, no campaign is detected within 500 alerts per day, and that the ordering of these detectors by ROC-AUC did not reflect their ordering by the budget needed to detect anything. They do not show that deep or graph-based detectors fail under the same protocol, because none has been evaluated, and they do not show that rules outperform learned detectors, because the AUC-PR difference between the rule engine and pair rarity is not significant and the rule engine benefits from labels.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:reproducibility}
The code, configuration and run outputs are public \parencite{authbenchsoftware}; the first version of this report is archived \parencite{authbenchpreprint}. The pipeline is staged (ingest, parse, label, split, features, detectors, evaluation), each stage a function over lazy data frames, under data version control. Continuous integration runs linting, strict type checking and the test suite on every change, with a coverage threshold on the evaluation, feature, ingestion and detector modules, and regenerates the synthetic results, which must match the committed ones byte for byte. Every number, table and figure of this report is generated from the committed run outputs by a script, which continuous integration also re-runs.

The evaluated configuration was produced by the code at commit \texttt{17e897c} on a rented machine with about 30\,GB of memory; its outputs were committed in \texttt{c641c4c}. The single-day configuration was produced on a machine with 8\,GB of memory and committed in \texttt{fc7b7de}. The split statistics and source concentrations of \cref{tab:split} were computed from the labelled partitions of that run with the same function the pipeline now calls at every run. Fitting is seeded throughout, and the bootstrap uses a fixed seed.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
On the LANL red-team data, under a leak-free protocol with adequate history, none of seven detectors detects a campaign within 500 alerts per day, the daily budget needed for a first detection spans two orders of magnitude, and two detectors with a ROC-AUC near 0.88 need more alerts than a random score. The ordering by ROC-AUC did not reflect the ordering by detection budget, whereas the ordering by AUC-PR was closer to it. Because the red-team activity is concentrated on one host and the catalogue is small, these are measurements on one operation, stated with their limits. The immediate continuations are the evaluation of deep and graph-based detectors, starting with temporal link prediction, under the same protocol; equal tuning budgets and fitted normalisation for all detectors; the use of the red-team activity of days 14 to 29 as a second test period; and a second labelled dataset.

\section*{Ethics and data statement}
The LANL data are public and de-identified, and their use requires citation of the provider; the benchmark manipulates no personal data. It is defensive in purpose and contains no offensive technique.
