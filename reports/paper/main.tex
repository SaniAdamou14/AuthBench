\begin{abstract}
\noindent Security operations centres investigate a bounded number of alerts per day, yet anomaly detectors for authentication logs are mostly compared by threshold-free metrics. We present \AuthBench{}, an open evaluation harness that enforces its temporal split in code, ranks events within each day, scores red-team campaigns at fixed daily alert budgets, and reports the exact daily budget at which a detector first detects a campaign together with the distribution of that budget under a random ranking. We apply it to seven classical detectors on the authentication log of the Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) data, testing on days 11 to 13 (\WideTestEvents{} events, \WideTestPositives{} red-team events, \WideTestCampaigns{} campaigns). No detector detects a campaign within 500 alerts per day. Only the rule engine and PCA reach their first detection outside the central 95\% range of a random ranking (\WideRulesFirstDetection{} and \WidePcaFirstDetection{} alerts per day); Isolation Forest and HBOS, with a ROC-AUC of \WideIForestRocAuc{} and \WideHbosRocAuc{}, are indistinguishable from it. Over the five non-trivial detectors, ROC-AUC orders them unlike the first-detection budget (Spearman $\rho = \WideSpearmanRocAuc$), whereas recall at a false-positive rate of $10^{-4}$ orders them closely ($\rho = \WideSpearmanRecallFpr$); with five detectors neither coefficient is significant, and the contrast rests on the one detector calibrated with labels. The labelled activity is a single intrusion launched mostly from one host, so these results are a case study of one operation, not an estimate for detectors in general.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

An anomaly detector deployed in an enterprise network is consumed through an alert queue. Each day, analysts investigate the highest-ranked alerts until their capacity is exhausted, and the detector is useful only if an attack appears within that capacity. Interviews with SOC practitioners describe alert streams dominated by false positives \parencite{alahmadi2022}; automated triage has been proposed to combat the resulting alert fatigue \parencite{hassan2019nodoze}, and a lateral-movement detector deployed in a real enterprise has been reported at fewer than nine alerts per day \parencite{ho2021hopper}. The base-rate argument of \textcite{axelsson2000} explains the constraint: when attacks are rare, even a small false-positive rate produces an alert stream dominated by false alarms.

Authentication logs are an extreme instance of this regime: in the test partition used here, the positive rate is $\WideTestRate$. Detectors for such data are nevertheless often summarised by the area under the ROC curve (ROC-AUC), which averages the ranking over every operating point, including those no SOC could staff. That ROC-AUC is uninformative about the extreme left of the curve is the classical motivation for partial and truncated ROC areas \parencite{mcclish1989} and for precision-recall analysis \parencite{davis2006,saito2015}, and it has been stated for the LANL data in particular \parencite{king2022euler}. Budget-based metrics have also been reported on LANL \parencite{larroche2021decades,garchery2020adsage,larroche2025ultralmd}. This paper does not claim the observation; it contributes a harness that makes the measurement reproducible and leak-free, and a case study whose limits are measured.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{An open evaluation harness} (\cref{sec:protocol,sec:evaluation}). It enforces the temporal split by code; ranks events within each day with a stated, label-free tie order and best- and worst-case tie brackets; computes, for each campaign, the daily budget at which it is first detected, which yields the whole campaign-recall curve; gives the exact distribution of the first-detection budget under a random ranking; and estimates uncertainty with a paired campaign-level bootstrap whose resolution and Monte Carlo limits are reported.
\item \textbf{A case study on LANL} (\cref{sec:results}) of seven classical detectors (two floors, a rule engine, two statistical scores and two outlier detectors), reported against the random-ranking null and with leave-one-detector-out analyses of the orderings induced by threshold-free and low-false-positive-rate metrics.
\item \textbf{Diagnostics that bound such case studies} (\cref{sec:diagnostics,sec:circularity,sec:threats}): the concentration of the red-team events on one host, the tiered and time-ordered ranking produced by window-relative rarity scores, the retrospective normalisation of the rule engine, and a control for circularity between a data generator and a rule.
\end{enumerate}
We do not evaluate deep or graph-based detectors, and our event universe and alert unit differ from those of prior budgeted evaluations on LANL (\cref{sec:comparison}); both points limit what the case study can say, and both are the object of ongoing work.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Evaluation pitfalls in security machine learning.}
\textcite{axelsson2000} established the base-rate fallacy for intrusion detection, and \textcite{sommer2010} argued that anomaly detection differs from the closed-world settings in which machine learning usually succeeds. \textcite{pendlebury2019} showed that spatial and temporal experimental bias inflates malware classification results. \textcite{arp2022} identified ten recurring pitfalls, among them data snooping, inappropriate performance measures and the base-rate fallacy, and \textcite{apruzzese2023sok} assessed the practical value of machine learning for network intrusion detection. Leakage in data mining is formalised by \textcite{kaufman2012leakage}.

\paragraph{Metrics under class imbalance.}
ROC-AUC is invariant to the class ratio, whereas precision is not; \textcite{davis2006} related the two curves, and \textcite{saito2015} showed empirically that the precision-recall plot is the more informative one on imbalanced data. Partial ROC areas restrict the analysis to low false-positive rates \parencite{mcclish1989}. None of these encodes a capacity per unit of time, which is what an alert budget imposes.

\paragraph{Detection on the LANL data.}
The LANL comprehensive cyber-security events data \parencite{kent2015dataset,kent2016chapter} pairs 58 days of enterprise authentication events with labelled red-team activity; its authentication graph is characterised by \textcite{kent2015authgraphs}. \textcite{turcotte2016} model users relative to peer groups by Poisson factorisation. \textcite{tuor2018} apply recurrent language models to log lines; their operational metric is the average percentile of malicious events in a daily ranking, not average precision, and \textcite{brown2018} add attention mechanisms to that family. \textcite{bowman2020} learn node embeddings on the authentication graph, training on 40 days without reported malicious activity and testing on the 18 days with it, a split that is not chronological; their best detector reports 635 true positives against 107{,}960 false positives. \textcite{king2022euler} cast lateral-movement detection as temporal link prediction (Euler), train on the snapshots that precede the first anomalous edge, and note that ROC-AUC is not a good indicator of model quality on data as imbalanced as LANL. \textcite{xu2024argus} (Argus), \textcite{paudel2022pikachu} (Pikachu), \textcite{amin2019cadence} (CADENCE) and \textcite{gokstorp2024} (transformer sequence models) propose further detectors. Outside LANL, \textcite{siadati2017} detect structurally anomalous logins in an enterprise network.

\paragraph{Budgeted evaluation and recent re-evaluations.}
Daily-budget recall was used by \textcite{tuor2017} on insider-threat data and adopted by \textcite{garchery2020adsage}, who report cumulative recall over budgets of investigated user-days on LANL (training on days 1--8, testing on days 9--13) and find that a rule flagging previously unseen source computers performs on par with their graph-based detector. \textcite{larroche2021decades} keep remote LogOn events whose destination is a user account, train on 8 days and evaluate on the next 5, and report the detection rate for a fixed daily budget of investigated events (DR-1K, 1{,}000 events per day, of 0.081 for their detector) alongside a truncated ROC area. \textcite{larroche2025ultralmd} reports ROC-AUC, average precision and the recall obtained when the top $B$ edges of each hourly window are investigated; in his comparison, higher ROC-AUC does not imply higher budgeted recall. Two 2026 studies re-evaluate published detectors under harmonised conditions. \textcite{larroche2026fair} harmonises the preprocessing and labelling of LANL and OpTC and shows that published average precision values do not survive (for Argus on LANL, 32.27 as originally reported against $0.09 \pm 0.04$ re-evaluated, both in percent in his Table~5); he notes that the labelled LANL authentications belong to a single intrusion, mostly from one source host, recommends training on the first 41 hours and testing on the remainder with all remote LogOn events, and concludes that the dataset should be used only for unsupervised methods. \textcite{zhao2026gids} decompose graph-based intrusion detectors into interchangeable stages and find that an encoder-free control ranks first by average precision on two of four datasets.

\paragraph{Positioning.}
\AuthBench{} scores individual authentication events, ranks them within each day, and counts detections per red-team campaign; it compares rules, statistical scores and classical outlier detectors under one leak-free protocol, and places each first-detection budget against its distribution under a random ranking. It differs from the works above in unit and universe, which makes its numbers not directly comparable with theirs (\cref{sec:comparison}).

\section{Data, labels and campaigns}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Source and event universe}
We use the authentication log of the LANL comprehensive cyber-security events data \parencite{kent2015dataset}: 1{,}051{,}430{,}459 events over 58 days from one enterprise Windows Active Directory environment, with one-second timestamps from an arbitrary epoch. The file is verified against a recorded SHA-256 digest before use. We keep every event of the log, of every authentication type and orientation, including machine accounts; missing authentication and logon types are encoded as indicators. This universe is broader than in prior budgeted work: in the test partition, \WideMachineSourceShare{} of events have a machine account as source user, and \WideMachineMalicious{} of the red-team events do. Failed authentications appear only for users who succeed at least once, so no conclusion is drawn about attacks on non-existent accounts.

\subsection{Labels and campaigns}
The red-team file contains 749 lines, of which 34 are exact duplicates, leaving 715 distinct red-team events. Authentication events are labelled malicious by an exact join on the four red-team fields: time, user (with domain), source computer and destination computer. Red-team activity spans days 1 to 29. We group red-team events into campaigns per user: consecutive events of the same user belong to one campaign when they are at most $g = 24$ hours apart. Campaigns are cut at partition boundaries; in the test partition, \WideTruncatedCampaigns{} campaigns continue into day 14, outside the test window.

\subsection{Concentration of the red-team activity}
\label{sec:concentration}
In the test partition, \WideTestTopHostPositives{} of the \WideTestPositives{} red-team events (\WideTestTopHostShare{}) and \WideTestTopHostCampaigns{} of the \WideTestCampaigns{} campaigns involve the same source computer, \TopHost{}; the shares are \WideValTopHostShare{} in validation and \WideTrainTopHostShare{} in training. This agrees with the observation of \textcite{larroche2026fair} that the labelled LANL authentications belong to a single simulated intrusion. Our campaigns are therefore accounts and phases of one operation, not independent attacks, and the effective number of independent intrusions in this evaluation is one.

Every partition must contain campaigns and the red team stops at day 29. Day 11 contains no red-team event, so the test positives fall on days 12 (\WideTestDayTwelvePositives{}) and 13 (\WideTestDayThirteenPositives{}). This split needs labelled validation data only for the rule engine, whose weights are calibrated on it. The alternative recommended by \textcite{larroche2026fair}, training on the first 41 hours and testing on the remainder, would place all red-team activity in test and use 28 attack-free days to estimate false positives; it is not the configuration evaluated here, and the red-team activity of days 14 to 29 is unused.

\subsection{Leakage control and its limits}
\label{sec:leakage}
Three mechanisms enforce the split. A guard raises an error unless the last training timestamp precedes the first validation timestamp and the last validation timestamp precedes the first test timestamp. A static architecture test fails if any module under \texttt{models/} or \texttt{features/} imports the only function that loads the test partition. Windowed features use causal primitives: rolling aggregates use the window $[t-w, t)$, and statistics that a running sum cannot express use a self-join restricted to earlier events of the same entity, earlier meaning earlier in (time, event identifier) order; a unit test builds events whose features would differ if the future were visible. The two fitted feature transformations, category frequencies and a night window, are fitted on training data only (the night window is computed but used by no detector).

These guarantees exclude labels and fitted quantities from crossing partitions; they do not make every score deployable as is. Two elements of the evaluation remain retrospective within the test partition. First, the daily top-$k$ is computed from all the events of a day, so a day's alert threshold uses events that occur later that day; a deployed detector would fix its threshold in advance. Second, the rule engine normalises each rule by its rank over the whole scored partition, so the score of an event on day 12 depends on events of day 13 (\cref{sec:detectors}).

\subsection{Features}
\label{sec:features}
Detectors that consume a feature vector receive 21 columns from four families, listed in \cref{app:features}: event-level indicators and training-fitted category frequencies (F1); per-user event counts and failure ratios over the preceding hour and day (F2); novelty and rarity of the (user, destination computer) pair (F3); and the time of day on the unit circle with the deviation of an event from its user's running circular mean (F4). Novelty is defined relative to the window a partition reads (the partition and its warm-up), not to the whole history. No feature describes the source computer of an authentication, although the source side carries the signal most often exploited on LANL \parencite{garchery2020adsage,bowman2020}.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Budgeted metrics}
Let $E_d$ be the events of test day $d$, $P$ the red-team events and $\mathcal{C}$ the campaigns, each a set $P_c \subseteq P$. A detector assigns a score $s(e)$ to every event. Within each day, events are ordered by decreasing score, ties being ordered by arrival (time, then event identifier), an order that is total and independent of labels. Let $r(e) \in \{1, \dots, |E_d|\}$ be the rank of $e$ in its day. The alert set at daily budget $k$ is $A(k) = \{\, e : r(e) \le k \,\}$, and event and campaign recall are
\begin{equation}
\Rev(k) = \frac{|A(k) \cap P|}{|P|}, \qquad
\Rcamp(k) = \frac{|\{\, c \in \mathcal{C} : P_c \cap A(k) \neq \emptyset \,\}|}{|\mathcal{C}|}.
\end{equation}
We report $k \in \{10, 50, 100, 500\}$. With the detection budget of a campaign defined as $b(c) = \min_{e \in P_c} r(e)$, a campaign is detected at budget $k$ exactly when $b(c) \le k$, so $\Rcamp(k)$ is the empirical distribution function of $\{b(c)\}$ at every $k$. We report the first-detection budget $b^{*} = \min_{c} b(c)$, the smallest daily budget at which a detector detects anything. It does not saturate at zero, but it is the minimum of the curve, determined by one campaign; the full set $\{b(c)\}$ is not part of the run outputs analysed here.

\paragraph{Random-ranking null.} For a uniformly random ranking, the positives of day $d$ occupy a uniformly random subset of $p_d$ ranks among $N_d$, so
\begin{equation}
\Pr\bigl(b^{*} > x\bigr) = \prod_{d} \binom{N_d - x}{p_d} \Big/ \binom{N_d}{p_d} = \prod_{d} \prod_{i=0}^{p_d - 1} \frac{N_d - x - i}{N_d - i}.
\end{equation}
With the test days' sizes ($N_{12} = \WideTestDayTwelveEvents$, $p_{12} = \WideTestDayTwelvePositives$; $N_{13} = \WideTestDayThirteenEvents$, $p_{13} = \WideTestDayThirteenPositives$), its median is \WideNullFirstDetectionMedian{} and its central 95\% range is [\WideNullFirstDetectionLow{}, \WideNullFirstDetectionHigh{}]. We report, for each detector, the null probability $\Pr(b^{*}_{\mathrm{random}} \le b^{*})$, and do not compare detectors with the single realisation of the random floor M0a.

\paragraph{Ties.} For coarse scores, which events enter $A(k)$ can depend on the tie order rather than the detector. We report $\Rcamp(k)$ under the least favourable order (benign first within each tied group), a lower bound, and a favourable order (malicious first, one per campaign before any second), with the number of tied events competing for the last places of each budget.

\subsection{Threshold-free and low-false-positive-rate metrics}
ROC-AUC averages the ranking over all operating points. The area under the precision-recall curve (AUC-PR), estimated by average precision over the test partition, weights each positive by the precision at its rank and is therefore dominated by the best-ranked positives. Recall at a false-positive rate of $10^{-4}$, computed over the whole test partition, concerns only the top of the ranking: it admits about 5{,}500 benign events over the three test days, roughly 1{,}800 per day. Global precision at $k \le 500$ and recall at a false-positive rate of $10^{-5}$ are also computed; they are zero for every detector.

\subsection{Uncertainty}
\label{sec:uncertainty}
Events of a campaign are dependent, so we resample blocks \parencite{efron1993,field2007}: the blocks are the campaigns and the individual benign events, each resample draws with replacement as many blocks as there are (so the number of campaigns varies across resamples), and every detector is evaluated on the same resamples. Resamples without positives are discarded and redrawn. With $R = 2000$ resamples, the interval of each detector is the 95\% percentile interval of its AUC-PR. For two detectors, the paired difference $\Delta^{*}$ yields the percentile-bootstrap sign statistic
\begin{equation}
p = \min\!\left(1, \frac{2\,(t+1)}{R+1}\right), \qquad t = \min\bigl(\#\{\Delta^{*} \le 0\}, \#\{\Delta^{*} \ge 0\}\bigr),
\end{equation}
which inverts the percentile interval rather than resampling under a null hypothesis; the 21 comparisons are corrected by the procedure of \textcite{holm1979}. When $t = 0$, $p$ equals its floor $2/(R+1)$, which is a Monte Carlo estimate, not a bound: with $t = 0$ out of 2000, the one-sided 95\% Clopper-Pearson bound on the underlying tail probability gives a Holm-adjusted value of at most \WideFloorMonteCarloUpper{}, above the 0.05 level; certifying the level at the floor would need about \WideResamplesForCertifiedFloor{} resamples. Holm's procedure cannot reject any of 21 hypotheses with fewer than \MinResamplesTwentyOne{} resamples, and any of the 28 of the full eight-detector catalogue with fewer than \MinResamplesTwentyEight{}. All intervals are conditional on the fitted scores: they do not include the variance of fitting on another sample or seed, and benign events are resampled as independent units although they cluster by user and host.

\subsection{No detection within 500 alerts per day}
No detector detects any of the \WideTestCampaigns{} campaigns at 10, 50, 100 or 500 alerts per day, under any order of tied scores. The result should be read against the null: a random ranking detects a campaign within 500 alerts with probability \WideNullWithinFiveHundred{}, so detecting one at that budget requires a ranking whose top is about a hundred times richer in red-team events than chance. Under independence of campaigns, zero detections out of \WideTestCampaigns{} would bound campaign recall at 500 alerts per day below \WideZeroRecallUpper{} (one-sided 95\%); because the campaigns belong to one operation, this bound says little about other intrusions.

\subsection{First-detection budgets against the random-ranking null}
The rule engine first detects a campaign at \WideRulesFirstDetection{} alerts per day and PCA at \WidePcaFirstDetection{}, below the 2.5th percentile of the null (\WideRulesNullPercentile{} and \WidePcaNullPercentile{}). Pair rarity, at \WidePairRarityFirstDetection{}, lies at the \WidePairRarityNullPercentile{} percentile, inside the central 95\% range. HBOS (\WideHbosFirstDetection{}, \WideHbosNullPercentile{}) and Isolation Forest (\WideIForestFirstDetection{}, \WideIForestNullPercentile{}) are indistinguishable from a random ranking at the top of the queue, although their ROC-AUC is \WideHbosRocAuc{} and \WideIForestRocAuc{} and their AUC-PR exceeds that of both floors at the resolution floor of the bootstrap (\cref{sec:stability}). Each $b^{*}$ is determined by a single campaign of a single operation.

\subsection{Orderings induced by threshold-free and low-false-positive-rate metrics}
\label{sec:orderings}
\Cref{tab:correlations} compares, over the five non-trivial detectors, the ordering induced by each metric with the ordering by first-detection budget. ROC-AUC gives $\rho = \WideSpearmanRocAuc$, AUC-PR $\rho = \WideSpearmanAucPr$ and recall at a false-positive rate of $10^{-4}$ $\rho = \WideSpearmanRecallFpr$. Exact permutation tests over all 120 orderings find none of these significant ($p = \WideSpearmanPRocAuc$, \WideSpearmanPAucPr{} and \WideSpearmanPRecallFpr{}), and the orderings of the first-detection budgets themselves carry no uncertainty estimate. The contrast between ROC-AUC and the other two metrics is attributable to one detector: without M1, ROC-AUC and AUC-PR order the remaining four detectors identically, and both give $\rho = \WideSpearmanRocAucWithoutRules$ (\cref{app:orderings}). M1 has the lowest ROC-AUC (\WideRulesRocAuc{}), the highest recall at a false-positive rate of $10^{-4}$ (\WideRulesRecallFpr{}) and the lowest first-detection budget, and it is the detector whose weights were chosen to maximise validation AUC-PR on the same operation. The agreement of AUC-PR and of low-false-positive-rate recall with the first-detection budget is close to definitional, since all three are governed by the best-ranked positives, whereas ROC-AUC is a mean-rank statistic.

\subsection{Stability of the AUC-PR ordering}
\label{sec:stability}
Of the \WidePairsTotal{} pairwise differences in AUC-PR, \WidePairsSignificant{} reach a Holm-adjusted value of \WideHolmFloor{} or less, all at the resolution floor (\cref{app:pairwise}); among the \WideNonTrivialPairs{} pairs of non-trivial detectors, \WideNonTrivialPairsSignificant{}. The three remaining differences are random against always-fail, PCA against HBOS, and pair rarity against the rule engine. Because the floor is a Monte Carlo estimate whose 95\% bound exceeds the level (\cref{sec:uncertainty}), and because every resample contains the same operation, we read these results as the stability of the AUC-PR ordering under resampling of the compromised accounts, not as significance with respect to other intrusions. The differences are also small in absolute terms (of order $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-4}$) for detectors that detect nothing within 500 alerts per day.

\subsection{Effect of history: the single-day configuration}
\label{sec:single-day}
An earlier configuration, constrained by an 8\,GB machine, used one day per partition (training on day 5, validation on day 8, testing on day 12) without warm-up (\cref{app:single-day}). In it, the rule engine detected 2 of 39 campaigns at 500 alerts per day (\OnedayRulesRecallAtFiveHundred{}); in the evaluated configuration it detects none. The difference is not significant (one-sided Fisher exact test, $p = \SingleDayFisherP$, ignoring that day 12 belongs to both test sets), the test window and the warm-up changed together, and the result is therefore reported as not reproduced rather than as an effect of history. ROC-AUC values changed little for pair rarity (\OnedayPairRarityRocAuc{} and \WidePairRarityRocAuc{}) and more for PCA (\OnedayPcaRocAuc{} and \WidePcaRocAuc{}).

\section{Diagnostics}
\label{sec:diagnostics}

\paragraph{Pair rarity ranks by novelty tier, then by time.} In M2a, the denominator $n$ counts all earlier events in the window and grows by a factor of at most about 1.4 within a test day, which changes $\log(n+1)$ by less than the gap between consecutive novelty tiers ($\log 2$ between $n_p = 0$ and $n_p = 1$). Within a day, M2a therefore ranks every event whose pair is new in the window above every event whose pair has been seen once, and those above every event whose pair has been seen twice (the gaps $\log 2$ and $\log 1.5$ both exceed the growth of $\log(n+1)$); within each of these tiers it ranks later events first. On day 12, \WideNewPairsDayTwelve{} pairs appear for the first time in the window (\WideNewPairsDayEleven{} on day 11, \WideNewPairsDayThirteen{} on day 13), and \WideMaliciousNewDayTwelve{} of the \WideTestDayTwelvePositives{} red-team events of that day fall on the first second of their pair. M2a's first-detection budget thus mostly reflects how many new pairs occurred later on the day of the attack than the red team's last new pair, a property of the attack's time of day. The zero-width tie brackets do not reveal this, because the time order is inside the score. The same feature enters PCA, Isolation Forest, HBOS and rule R5.

\paragraph{The source side is absent.} \TopHost{} is the source of \WideTestTopHostShare{} of the test positives, yet no feature describes the source computer (\cref{sec:features}). A label-free rule on source-computer novelty performed on par with a graph detector on LANL \parencite{garchery2020adsage}; its absence from the catalogue is a limitation of the case study, not a result.

\section{A control for circularity}
\label{sec:circularity}

Before the LANL run, the protocol was exercised on a synthetic sample whose generator emits each campaign as a chain: a user authenticates from $A$ to $B$, then from $B$ to $C$. On that sample, the rule engine detects all four test campaigns at 10 alerts per day. A decomposition by rule (\cref{tab:rules}) explains why: rule R7, which tests whether a user's previous destination equals the current source within 30 minutes, is the generator's own predicate; it separates malicious from benign events by a factor of \DemoRSevenRatio{} and receives a calibrated weight of \DemoRSevenWeight{}. Against the LANL red team, the same engine detects no campaign within 500 alerts per day. A synthetic sample written by the author of the rules cannot distinguish a good heuristic from one informed by the generator. The control rests on four synthetic test campaigns, and the corresponding rule-level decomposition on LANL has not yet been computed.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\paragraph{One operation.} The labelled activity is one intrusion, mostly from one host (\cref{sec:concentration}). The bootstrap treats campaigns as independent and therefore describes variability across the accounts of that operation; generalisation to other intrusions is not established.

\paragraph{Labels and calibration.} M1 calibrates its weights on validation labels of the same operation; no other detector uses labels, and their hyperparameters are defaults. \textcite{larroche2026fair} advises against supervised use of these labels. A label-free variant of M1 and equal tuning budgets are needed before comparing M1 with the other detectors.

\paragraph{Retrospective elements.} The daily top-$k$ uses all events of a day, and M1's rank normalisation spans the three test days (\cref{sec:leakage}).

\paragraph{Features.} Training days 0 and 1 have no history, so the fitting distribution of the outlier detectors differs from the test distribution, which has a warm-up. Novelty is window-relative and pair rarity is tiered and time-ordered (\cref{sec:diagnostics}). No source-side feature is included. For the first event of a user in the window, the deviation from the user's typical hour is set to zero, the most typical value.

\paragraph{Universe, unit and budget.} All authentication events are ranked, including the \WideMachineSourceShare{} with a machine-account source, and the alert unit is the raw event; SOC tooling usually aggregates alerts by entity, and prior LANL work uses LogOn events, edges or user-days. The budgets of 10 to 500 events per day are not derived from a staffing model.

\paragraph{Uncertainty.} The intervals are conditional on one fit and treat benign events as independent; the floor $p$-values are Monte Carlo estimates not certified at the 0.05 level with 2000 resamples; the first-detection budgets carry no interval; and the orderings rest on five detectors.

\paragraph{Campaign definition and rule mapping.} Campaign recall depends on the grouping threshold $g$, whose sensitivity has not been analysed, and counts each compromised account of the operation as a campaign. The ATT\&CK techniques attached to the rules (\cref{app:rules}) express design intent and are not validated against ground truth.

\paragraph{External validity.} One enterprise network observed in 2015, before widespread multi-factor authentication; labels are a partial ground truth; and the red team did not attempt to evade these detectors.

\section{Scope of the claims}
\label{sec:scope}
For the seven detectors, the universe and the protocol above, on the test partition of one network and one operation, no campaign is detected within 500 alerts per day; two detectors reach a first detection earlier than a random ranking would, two do not, and their ROC-AUC did not indicate which. Beyond this catalogue, the results do not show that ROC-AUC fails to predict budgeted detection, that budgeted detection fails on LANL, that deep or graph-based detectors fail under this protocol, or that rules outperform learned detectors.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:reproducibility}
The code, configuration and run outputs are public \parencite{authbenchsoftware}; the first version of this report is archived \parencite{authbenchpreprint}. Continuous integration runs linting, strict type checking and the test suite on every change, with a coverage threshold on the evaluation, feature, ingestion and detector modules, regenerates the synthetic results byte for byte, and regenerates every number and table of this report from the committed run outputs. The evaluated configuration was produced by the code at commit \texttt{17e897c} on a rented machine with about 30\,GB of memory; its outputs were committed in \texttt{c641c4c}. The split statistics, per-day sizes and diagnostics of \cref{tab:split,sec:diagnostics} were computed from the labelled partitions of that run, with the function the pipeline now calls at every run and with a script published alongside. The single-day configuration was produced on a machine with 8\,GB of memory and committed in \texttt{fc7b7de}. Fitting and resampling are seeded.

\section{Conclusion and ongoing work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Under a leak-free protocol, none of seven classical detectors detects a campaign of the LANL red team within 500 alerts per day; the rule engine and PCA reach a first detection earlier than a random ranking would, Isolation Forest and HBOS do not, and their ROC-AUC did not distinguish these cases. Because the labelled activity is one operation, the catalogue is small and the universe differs from prior work, these findings describe one case. The next steps follow from the review of this version: the split recommended by \textcite{larroche2026fair}, which tests on all red-team activity after the first 41 hours; source-side features and the source-novelty rule; the full distribution of per-campaign detection budgets with intervals; alert units by event, edge and user-day; a label-free rule engine with training-fitted normalisation; multi-seed fitting and a benign-clustered bootstrap with enough resamples; and the evaluation of a temporal link-prediction detector in the style of \textcite{king2022euler} under the same protocol.

\section*{Ethics and data statement}
The LANL data are public and de-identified, and their use requires citation of the provider; the benchmark manipulates no personal data. It is defensive in purpose and contains no offensive technique.
