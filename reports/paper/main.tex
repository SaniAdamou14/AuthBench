\begin{abstract}
\noindent Security operations centres investigate a bounded number of alerts per day, yet anomaly detectors for authentication logs are mostly compared by threshold-free metrics. We present \AuthBench{}, an open harness that enforces its temporal split in code, ranks events within each day, gives every red-team campaign the daily budget at which it is first detected, and places each result against its exact distribution under a random ranking. We evaluate thirteen detectors, from rules and rarity scores to two autoencoders and a reimplementation of the temporal graph detector Euler, on the LANL authentication log under the protocol of \textcite{larroche2026fair}, whose event set we reproduce exactly, and under a day-based split. Ranked as individual events, no detector detects a campaign within 10 alerts per day and none exceeds \TempPcaRecallFiveHundred{} of the campaigns at 500, although at 100 alerts per day the autoencoder alerts on the intrusion's first day. ROC-AUC tracks the bulk of the campaign-recall curve (Spearman $\rho = \HarmRhoRocAucMedian$ and $\TempRhoRocAucMedian$ with the median campaign budget) but not its leading edge: it does not rank the detectors by first detection ($\rho = \HarmRhoRocAucFirst$ and $\TempRhoRocAucFirst$), and the earliest first detections depend on the fitting seed and on a partial first test day. Ranked as user-days, Euler and a source-novelty score reach about half of the campaigns at 500 per day, against \HarmUnitNullFiveHundred{} expected from a random ranking, at a higher cost per alert. Rerun on the same machine with its released preprocessing, the authors' Euler code reproduces its published AUC (\EulerAuthorsAuc{} against \EulerPublishedAuc{}) but not its average precision (\EulerAuthorsAp{} against \EulerPublishedAp{}). The labelled activity is one intrusion, \RedTeamTopHostShare{} of it from one host: these results are a case study of one operation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

An anomaly detector deployed in an enterprise network is consumed through an alert queue. Each day, analysts investigate the highest-ranked alerts until their capacity is exhausted, and the detector is useful only if an attack appears within that capacity. Interviews with SOC practitioners describe alert streams dominated by false positives \parencite{alahmadi2022}; automated triage has been proposed to combat the resulting alert fatigue \parencite{hassan2019nodoze}, and a lateral-movement detector evaluated on fifteen months of enterprise logins generated fewer than nine alerts per day \parencite{ho2021hopper}. The base-rate argument of \textcite{axelsson2000} explains the constraint: when attacks are rare, even a small false-positive rate produces an alert stream dominated by false alarms.

Authentication logs are an extreme instance of this regime: in the harmonised test partition used here, the positive rate is $\HarmTestRate$. Detectors for such data are nevertheless often summarised by the area under the ROC curve (ROC-AUC), which averages the ranking over every operating point, including those no SOC could staff. That ROC-AUC is uninformative about the extreme left of the curve is the classical motivation for partial ROC areas \parencite{mcclish1989} and precision-recall analysis \parencite{davis2006,saito2015}, and it has been stated for the LANL data in particular \parencite{king2022euler}. Budget-based metrics have also been reported on LANL \parencite{larroche2021decades,garchery2020adsage,larroche2025ultralmd}, and harmonised re-evaluations of published detectors now exist \parencite{larroche2026fair,zhao2026gids,wang2025rr}. This paper does not claim the observation; it contributes a harness that makes the measurement reproducible and leak-free, and a case study whose limits are measured rather than assumed.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{An open evaluation harness} (\cref{sec:protocol,sec:evaluation}). It enforces the temporal split by code and tests that every detector scores an event identically alone or among others; ranks events, edge-hours and user-days within each day with a stated, label-free tie order; gives every campaign its detection budget, hence the whole campaign-recall curve, and the exact distribution of the first-detection budget under a random ranking, for events and for user-days; and reports a paired account-level bootstrap, a bootstrap over source users, the variance of refitting with other seeds, a fixed-threshold deployment, and a staffing model for the budgets.
\item \textbf{A case study on LANL} (\cref{sec:results}) of thirteen detectors under the protocol recommended by \textcite{larroche2026fair}, on exactly his event set, and under a day-based split with and without red-team events in training, read at the level of campaigns and of the intrusion as a whole.
\item \textbf{A reproduction of Euler} (\cref{sec:euler}) with the authors' own code run beside our reimplementation, and an edge-by-edge reconciliation of the two test sets with the published counts.
\item \textbf{Diagnostics that bound the case study} (\cref{sec:diagnostics,sec:circularity}): the concentration of the red-team events on one host, a rule-by-rule decomposition of the rule engine on LANL, and a control for circularity between a data generator and a rule.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Evaluation pitfalls in security machine learning.}
\textcite{axelsson2000} established the base-rate fallacy for intrusion detection, and \textcite{sommer2010} argued that anomaly detection differs from the closed-world settings in which machine learning usually succeeds. \textcite{pendlebury2019} showed that spatial and temporal experimental bias inflates malware classification results. \textcite{arp2022} identified ten recurring pitfalls, among them data snooping, inappropriate performance measures and the base-rate fallacy, and \textcite{apruzzese2023sok} assessed the practical value of machine learning for network intrusion detection. Leakage in data mining is formalised by \textcite{kaufman2012leakage}.

\paragraph{Metrics under class imbalance.}
ROC-AUC is invariant to the class ratio, whereas precision is not; \textcite{davis2006} related the two curves, and \textcite{saito2015} showed empirically that the precision-recall plot is the more informative one on imbalanced data. Partial ROC areas restrict the analysis to low false-positive rates \parencite{mcclish1989}. None of these encodes a capacity per unit of time, which is what an alert budget imposes.

\paragraph{Detection on the LANL data.}
The LANL comprehensive cyber-security events data \parencite{kent2015dataset,kent2016chapter} pairs 58 days of enterprise authentication events with labelled red-team activity; its authentication graph is characterised by \textcite{kent2015authgraphs}. \textcite{turcotte2016} model users relative to peer groups by Poisson factorisation. \textcite{tuor2018} apply recurrent language models to log lines and report ROC-AUC together with the average percentile of malicious events in a daily ranking, which is not average precision; \textcite{brown2018} add attention mechanisms to that family. \textcite{bowman2020} learn node embeddings on the authentication graph, training on 40 days without reported malicious activity and testing on the 18 days with it, a split that is not chronological. \textcite{king2022euler,king2023euler} cast lateral-movement detection as temporal link prediction (Euler), train on the snapshots that precede the first anomalous edge, and note that ROC-AUC is not a good indicator of model quality on data as imbalanced as LANL. \textcite{xu2024argus} (Argus), \textcite{khoury2024jbeil} (Jbeil), \textcite{paudel2022pikachu} (Pikachu), \textcite{amin2019cadence} (CADENCE) and \textcite{gokstorp2024} (transformer sequence models) propose further detectors. Outside LANL, \textcite{siadati2017} detect structurally anomalous logins in an enterprise network.

\paragraph{Budgeted evaluation and re-evaluations.}
Daily-budget recall was used by \textcite{tuor2017} on insider-threat data and adopted by \textcite{garchery2020adsage}, who report on LANL a normalised cumulative recall over budgets of investigated user-days (an area under the daily recall curve), training on their days 1--8 and testing on days 9--13 with system accounts removed, and find that a rule flagging previously unseen source computers performs on par with their graph-based detector. Their days are counted from one: their 50 training anomalies are the 50 red-team events of our days 0--7. \textcite{larroche2021decades} model local and remote LogOn events whose destination is a user account, together with process creations, train on 8 days and evaluate on the next 5, and report the share of red-team events detected when the top $B$ events of each day are investigated (DR-1K: 0.081 for DECADES, 0.093 for CADENCE). \textcite{larroche2025ultralmd} reports ROC-AUC, average precision and the recall obtained when the top $B$ edges of each hourly window are investigated; in his comparison, higher ROC-AUC does not imply higher budgeted recall. \textcite{larroche2026fair} harmonises the preprocessing and labelling of LANL and OpTC, recommends training on the first 41 hours and testing on all remote LogOn events of the rest, notes that NTLM-only filtering makes detection easier because the LANL lateral movements happen to use NTLM, and re-evaluates Euler, Argus and Pikachu at the event level: published average precision values do not survive (for Argus, 32.27 as reported against $0.09 \pm 0.04$, in percent). He also notes that the labelled LANL authentications are one intrusion, mostly from one source host, so that detecting some of them may be enough, and that the dataset should be used only for unsupervised methods. \textcite{zhao2026gids} decompose graph-based intrusion detectors into interchangeable stages, define a campaign detection rate (the share of campaigns with at least one flagged event), report its range across seeds (42.1 percentage points for Argus on LANL), and find that an encoder-free control ranks first by average precision on two of four datasets. \textcite{wang2025rr} and \textcite{boucek2025replication} re-run published graph-based intrusion and lateral-movement detectors.

\paragraph{Positioning.}
\AuthBench{} reuses the event set of \textcite{larroche2026fair} and the campaign view of \textcite{zhao2026gids}, and adds what neither reports: daily alert budgets in three units with their exact random-ranking null, the first-detection budget of every campaign, the day on which the intrusion is first noticed at a given staffing, and the effect of the fitting seed and of a deployed threshold on these budgets. Its catalogue does not include Argus, Jbeil, UltraLMD++ or a sequence model; \cref{sec:comparison} places its numbers beside theirs on the same events.

\section{Data, labels and campaigns}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Source}
We use the authentication log of the LANL comprehensive cyber-security events data \parencite{kent2015dataset}: 1{,}051{,}430{,}459 events over 58 days from one enterprise Windows Active Directory environment, with one-second timestamps from an arbitrary epoch. The official server refused connections when this run was prepared, so the files were taken from a public mirror; their compressed bytes differ from the official digests, and their content was verified partially: the total row count is the published one, and three of the 58 days have the row counts and order-independent content hashes of a conversion of the official files. Missing authentication and logon types are encoded as indicators. Failed authentications appear only for users who succeed at least once, so no conclusion is drawn about attacks on non-existent accounts.

\subsection{Labels and campaigns}
The red-team file contains 749 lines, of which 34 are exact duplicates, leaving \RedTeamRows{} distinct red-team events. Authentication events are labelled malicious by an exact join on the four red-team fields: time, user (with domain), source computer and destination computer; \RedTeamUnmatched{} red-team events have no authentication event at the same second between the same computers and cannot be labelled by any pipeline (\cref{sec:euler}). Red-team activity spans days 1 to 29. We group red-team events into campaigns per user: consecutive events of the same user belong to one campaign when they are at most $g = 24$ hours apart, and campaigns are cut at partition boundaries. In the harmonised test, \HarmCampaignSingletons{} of the \HarmTestCampaigns{} campaigns hold a single event, the largest holds \HarmCampaignLargest{}, and \HarmBusiestDaysCampaigns{} touch the three busiest days (day and campaigns: \HarmBusiestDays{}).

\subsection{Concentration of the red-team activity}
\label{sec:concentration}
Of the \RedTeamRows{} distinct red-team events, \RedTeamTopHostEvents{} (\RedTeamTopHostShare{}) start from one source computer, \TopHost{}, and only \RedTeamSourceComputers{} source computers appear at all; in the day-based test partition the share is \TempTestTopHostShare{} of the labelled positives and \TempTestTopHostCampaigns{} of the \TempTestCampaigns{} campaigns. This agrees with the observation of \textcite{larroche2026fair} that the labelled LANL authentications belong to a single simulated intrusion. Our campaigns are therefore accounts and phases of one operation, not independent attacks, and the effective number of independent intrusions in this evaluation is one. We report both the share of campaigns detected, which measures coverage of the operation's accounts, and the day on which the operation is first noticed, which is what a SOC needs to detect once (\cref{sec:intrusion}).

\section{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\subsection{Two protocols}
\label{sec:split}
\Cref{tab:split} gives both protocols. The \emph{harmonised} protocol follows \textcite{larroche2026fair}: training on hours 0 to 41, which end before the first red-team event (hour 41.9), and testing on every later hour of the data, with every event compared with all earlier events from day 0 and the universe restricted to remote LogOn events (source and destination computers differ). Its training and test partitions hold \HarmUniverseEvents{} events and \HarmTestPositives{} positives, exactly the LANL event set of \textcite[Table~4]{larroche2026fair}. It has no validation partition, so no detector is tuned and no label is read anywhere. Its first test day holds only hours 41 to 47 (\HarmDayOneEvents{} events, against \HarmOtherDaysMin{} to \HarmOtherDaysMax{} on the other days), so a daily budget is a larger share of that day; we report every first detection with and without it. The \emph{temporal} protocol trains on days 0--7, validates on days 8--10 and tests on days 11--13, keeps every authentication event, and lets each partition read the two preceding days as warm-up; its partitions are those of the previous version of this report, which the event counts confirm (\cref{app:earlier}). The two protocols differ in split and in universe at once. Day 11 holds no red-team event, so the temporal test positives fall on days 12 (\TempTestDayTwelvePositives{}) and 13 (\TempTestDayThirteenPositives{}).

\paragraph{Training regimes.} With training data that contain red-team events (temporal training holds \TempTrainPositives{}), a detector can be fitted with them, the situation of a defender who does not know the network is compromised (R1, reported), or without them (R2). Features are computed with the red-team events in both regimes, because the history an event is compared against cannot be cleaned by a defender who has not found the attack. In the harmonised protocol the two regimes coincide.

\subsection{Leakage control and row-locality}
\label{sec:leakage}
By leak-free we mean temporally leak-free: no label, fitted quantity or future event crosses a partition boundary. A guard raises an error unless the last training timestamp precedes the first validation timestamp and the last validation timestamp precedes the first test timestamp. A static architecture test fails if any module under \texttt{models/} or \texttt{features/} imports the only function that loads the test partition. Windowed features use causal primitives over $[t-w, t)$, and every quantity fitted for features or detectors (category frequencies, rule normalisations, empirical distributions, thresholds) is fitted on training data, or on validation for tuning.

The test partition is scored one day at a time, so each detector must give an event the same score alone, in any chunk and in the whole partition. A unit test asserts this to the last bit for every detector, and every scored day re-checks it on the positives: a tolerance would hide a one-ULP difference that moves an event across a tie. Euler is causal at a coarser grain: an event is scored from the embeddings of the previous half hour, which include earlier test edges. One element remains retrospective within a day: the daily top-$k$ is computed from all the events of the day; \cref{sec:fixed} replaces it by a threshold fixed in advance.

\paragraph{Design history.} Temporal leakage is not the only kind. The previous version of this report was evaluated on the temporal test days, and its reviewers saw that run's results. The source-side features (M2c and rule R8), the edge-hour and user-day units and the anchoring of rarity to the start of the day were added afterwards, partly in response; the temporal test days have therefore been read by two code versions (\cref{app:earlier}), and they lie inside the harmonised test window. The harmonised protocol and its 57 test days were evaluated once, with the code of this version. Results that depend on these later choices, above all the user-day results of source rarity, are design choices informed by an earlier look at the same operation, not confirmatory evidence.

\subsection{Features}
\label{sec:features}
Detectors that consume a feature vector receive 25 columns from four families (\cref{app:features}): event-level indicators and training-fitted category frequencies (F1); per-user event counts and failure ratios over the preceding hour and day (F2); novelty and rarity of three relations, (user, destination), (user, source) and (source, destination) (F3); and the time of day with the deviation of an event from its user's running circular mean (F4). Rarity is $-\log\bigl((k+1)/(n+1)\bigr)$ with $k$ the earlier occurrences of the relation and $n$ counted on days before the event's day, so equally novel events of one day tie instead of being ordered by their time. Features are computed one day at a time with the state carried from day to day, and equal those computed over the whole window to the last bit.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Budgeted metrics}
Let $E_d$ be the events of test day $d$, $P$ the red-team events and $\mathcal{C}$ the campaigns, each a set $P_c \subseteq P$. A detector assigns a score $s(e)$ to every event. Within each day, events are ordered by decreasing score, ties being ordered by arrival (time, then event identifier), an order that is total and independent of labels. Let $r(e)$ be the rank of $e$ in its day. The alert set at daily budget $k$ is $A(k) = \{\, e : r(e) \le k \,\}$, and event and campaign recall are
\begin{equation}
\Rev(k) = \frac{|A(k) \cap P|}{|P|}, \qquad
\Rcamp(k) = \frac{|\{\, c \in \mathcal{C} : P_c \cap A(k) \neq \emptyset \,\}|}{|\mathcal{C}|}.
\end{equation}
With the detection budget of a campaign $b(c) = \min_{e \in P_c} r(e)$, a campaign is detected at budget $k$ exactly when $b(c) \le k$, so $\Rcamp$ is the empirical distribution function of $\{b(c)\}$ (\cref{fig:recall}). We report $k \in \{10, 50, 100, 500\}$; the first-detection budget $b^{*} = \min_{c} b(c)$, the smallest daily budget at which a detector detects anything, with the next two $b(c)$; the 10\%, 25\% and 50\% quantiles of $\{b(c)\}$ and the area under the campaign-recall curve on a logarithmic budget axis (log-area); and, because the campaigns are one operation, the first test day on which some red-team event is in the top $k$.

\paragraph{Random-ranking null.} For a uniformly random ranking, the positives of day $d$ occupy a uniformly random subset of $p_d$ ranks among $N_d$, so
\begin{equation}
\Pr\bigl(b^{*} > x\bigr) = \prod_{d} \binom{N_d - x}{p_d} \Big/ \binom{N_d}{p_d}.
\end{equation}
Its median is \HarmNullFirstDetectionMedian{} alerts per day in the harmonised test and \TempNullFirstDetectionMedian{} in the temporal test, with central 95\% ranges [\HarmNullFirstDetectionLow{}, \HarmNullFirstDetectionHigh{}] and [\TempNullFirstDetectionLow{}, \TempNullFirstDetectionHigh{}]. For each detector we report the exact probability $\Pr(b^{*}_{\mathrm{random}} \le b^{*})$, raw and Holm-adjusted over the non-trivial detectors, rather than comparing with the single realisation of the random floor M0a. The same product, restricted to the days up to $d$ with $x = k$, gives the probability that a random ranking notices the intrusion by day $d$.

\paragraph{Ties.} For coarse scores, which events enter $A(k)$ can depend on the tie order rather than the detector. We report $\Rcamp(k)$ under the stated arrival order with a bracket: the least favourable order (benign first within each tied group) and an achievable favourable one, with the number of tied events competing for the last places.

\paragraph{Alert units.} Analysts rarely triage single events. Every run also ranks two coarser units per day: the \emph{edge-hour} (source computer, destination computer, hour) and the \emph{user-day} (source user). A unit takes the score of its highest event; equal units are ordered by their first event's time, then by key, which reads no label, and no tie bracket is computed for units. A campaign is detected at budget $k$ in a unit when one of its units ranks in the top $k$ of its day. Because two campaigns of one account are more than a day apart, a positive user-day belongs to exactly one campaign (\HarmPositiveUserDays{} in the harmonised test), so Eq.~2 applies to user-days with $N_d$ the day's user-days; the expected campaign recall of a random ranking follows by drawing, per day, a hypergeometric number of positive user-days into the top $k$. The positive edge-hours are not in the run outputs, so edge-hour results have no null. Units are fewer than events (between \HarmUserDaysMin{} and \HarmUserDaysMax{} user-days per harmonised test day), so the same $k$ covers a larger share of each day, and investigating a user-day costs more than dismissing one event.

\paragraph{What a budget costs.} With 2.7 hours of triage per analyst and day \parencite{vectra2023} and 10 to 20 minutes per alert \parencite{li2026sheragent}, 10 alerts per day take \StaffTenLow{} to \StaffTenHigh{} analysts, 100 take \StaffHundredLow{} to \StaffHundredHigh{}, and 500 take \StaffFiveHundredLow{} to \StaffFiveHundredHigh{}, about a third of the alerts a typical SOC handles in a day across all its tools. Both inputs are weak (a vendor-commissioned survey and a self-reported preprint) and price every unit alike, but they place 10 alerts per day at one analyst and 500 at a team, not at equally plausible operating points; the low end agrees with the fewer than nine alerts per day of \textcite{ho2021hopper}.

\subsection{Threshold-free and low-false-positive-rate metrics}
ROC-AUC averages the ranking over all operating points. The area under the precision-recall curve (AUC-PR), estimated by average precision over the test partition, weights each positive by the precision at its rank and is therefore dominated by the best-ranked positives; its campaign-weighted variant gives each campaign the same total weight. Recall at a false-positive rate of $10^{-4}$, computed over the whole test partition, concerns only the top of the ranking.

\subsection{Uncertainty}
\label{sec:uncertainty}
Events of a campaign are dependent, and two campaigns of one account are not independent either, so we resample blocks \parencite{efron1993,field2007}: compromised accounts, each with all its red-team events, and individual benign events, drawn with replacement. Every detector shares the positive-block draws; each draws its benign multiplicities from its own score bins, which keeps the comparisons paired in the campaign component that carries the variance. Resamples without positives are discarded and redrawn. With $R = \num{10000}$ resamples, each detector's AUC-PR receives a BCa interval \parencite{efron1987} whose acceleration comes from the exact jackknife over every resampling unit, and each pairwise difference a percentile interval and the percentile-interval inversion statistic
\begin{equation}
p = \min\!\left(1, \frac{2\,(t+1)}{R+1}\right), \qquad t = \min\bigl(\#\{\Delta^{*} \le 0\}, \#\{\Delta^{*} \ge 0\}\bigr),
\end{equation}
corrected over the 78 pairs by the procedure of \textcite{holm1979}. Most significant pairs sit at the resolution floor $2/(R+1)$ (\HarmPairsAtFloor{} of \HarmPairsSignificant{} harmonised, \TempPairsAtFloor{} of \TempPairsSignificant{} temporal), so their $p$-values are bounds, not measurements. The first-detection budget is a minimum, for which the bootstrap is inconsistent; we do not report a bootstrap interval for it, and give instead its exact null, the next campaign budgets and its distribution over fitting seeds.

Two variances escape this bootstrap. The benign events of one user are correlated, so the analysis is repeated with source users as the resampling unit, every event of a user moving with it. And the fitted detectors are held fixed: M1, PCA, Isolation Forest, ECOD and HBOS are refitted with five seeds, their tuned configuration fixed. The autoencoders and Euler are fitted once.

\subsection{A fixed threshold}
\label{sec:fixed}
No SOC tool ranks a day after it ends. For each budget $k$, the threshold at which the calibration partition raised $k$ alerts per day on average is applied to the test partition, where the alert volume then varies from day to day. Calibration uses validation in the temporal protocol and training in the harmonised one; it reads volumes, never labels.

\section{Euler: reproduction and reconciliation}
\label{sec:euler}

\paragraph{Fidelity check.} We ran our reimplementation under the paper's own protocol: training before the first red-team event, edges scored per 30-minute snapshot, and edge-level AUC and average precision (AP) compared with table VI of \textcite{king2022euler} (GCN with GRU, link prediction: AUC \EulerPublishedAuc{}, AP \EulerPublishedAp{}, TPR \EulerPublishedTpr{}, FPR \EulerPublishedFpr{}, precision \EulerPublishedPrecision{}). \Cref{tab:euler} gives four configurations over five seeds each: all events; NTLM authentications only, as the authors' loader keeps, a filter that \textcite{larroche2026fair} notes makes detection easier because the LANL lateral movements use NTLM; that subset with the authors' node vocabulary (every computer has its own identifier, including those first seen after training); and with their default learning rate and patience. None reaches the published values. The vocabulary configuration drops to AUC \EulerFidNtlmVocabularyAuc{}, although the authors' code uses the same vocabulary, so some component of the reimplementation interacts badly with untrained node rows; we have not isolated which.

\paragraph{The authors' code.} We then ran the authors' code (\texttt{iHeartGraph/Euler}, commit \texttt{b6ab8fd}), edited only where its instructions ask for paths, with the dependencies listed in its \texttt{requirements.txt} (added in November 2023, after the paper) installed with pip rather than conda, on the same machine and data, with 4 workers as the paper states (its script defaults to 8), five repetitions. The code sets no random seed. It reproduces the published AUC closely (\EulerAuthorsAuc{} $\pm$ \EulerAuthorsAucSd{}; one-sample $t = \EulerAuthorsAucT$, $p = \EulerAuthorsAucTP$) but not the published AP: \EulerAuthorsAp{} $\pm$ \EulerAuthorsApSd{}, between \EulerAuthorsApMin{} and \EulerAuthorsApMax{} over the five runs ($t = \EulerAuthorsApT$, $p = \EulerAuthorsApTP$); its TPR (\EulerAuthorsTpr{}), FPR (\EulerAuthorsFpr{}) and precision (\EulerAuthorsPrecision{}) also fall short of the published row. What was not reproduced is the released code with its released preprocessing in that environment, not necessarily the published run: the released preprocessing keeps \EulerAuthorsNtlmLines{} NTLM lines (\EulerAuthorsNtlmNoLoops{} without self-loops) and \EulerAuthorsNodes{} nodes, against \EulerPublishedEvents{} events and \EulerPublishedNodes{} nodes in the paper's table V. At this prevalence a few positives decide AP: the four anomalous edges the released pipeline loses (below), if the published run scored them near the top, could account for most of the gap. The authors also compute AP on float32 scores, which saturate at the top of the ranking, and on a differently aligned edge set; we have not measured either effect.

\paragraph{Undocumented choices.} The authors' code differs from the paper in ways the paper does not describe, and our reimplementation follows the paper where it is explicit: edge weights $\sigma(\lfloor C/\Sigma \rfloor)$ instead of the paper's standardised $\sigma((C - \mu)/\Sigma)$, which gives most edges the weight 0.5; identity node features without the paper's node-type vector; ten negative samples per edge, drawn among non-edges; early stopping on the sum of validation AUC and AP; dropout 0.25 with edge dropout keeping 20\% of edges; one optimiser step per epoch over the whole training sequence; and the last 5\% of pre-attack time held out to set the threshold and the initial state of the test. Choices of ours where the paper is silent: as many negatives as edges, early stopping on a held-out loss, ReLU after the first layer (from the code), dropout 0.1, learning rate 0.01, truncated back-propagation over one day, and one shared node for computers unseen in training. Two deviations are ours and unintended: the second layer's bias is added before propagation, so it is scaled by node degree, and a self-loop is added to every node even where the graph already has one. Which of these differences closes the gap is not tested here.

\section{A control for circularity}
\label{sec:circularity}

Before the LANL runs, the protocol was exercised on a synthetic sample whose generator emits each campaign as a chain: a user authenticates from $A$ to $B$, then from $B$ to $C$. On that sample, the rule engine detects all four test campaigns at 10 alerts per day. A decomposition by rule (\cref{tab:rules}) explains why: rule R7, which tests whether a user's previous destination equals the current source within 30 minutes, is the generator's own predicate; it separates malicious from benign events by a factor of \DemoRSevenRatio{} and receives a calibrated weight of \DemoRSevenWeight{}. On LANL, the same rule ranks the red team at or below chance (\cref{tab:rules-lanl}). A synthetic sample written by the author of the rules cannot distinguish a good heuristic from one informed by the generator.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\paragraph{One operation, one dataset.} The labelled activity is one intrusion, mostly from one host (\cref{sec:concentration}), in one dataset. The bootstrap resamples the accounts of that operation; generalisation to other intrusions is not established, and every $p$-value should be read as stability under resampling of those accounts. A second dataset with several intrusions is needed before any claim about detectors in general.

\paragraph{Catalogue.} Argus, Jbeil, UltraLMD++, the authors' Euler and any sequence model are absent from the harness; our Euler is a reimplementation that stays below the authors' code on its own protocol (\cref{sec:euler}). The conclusions are about this catalogue.

\paragraph{Labels, tuning and design.} In the temporal protocol, four detectors are tuned on validation labels of the same operation, which \textcite{larroche2026fair} advises against, and the autoencoders, ECOD and Euler are not tuned; the harmonised protocol, which reads no label, is the label-free evidence. Some catalogue and unit choices were made after an earlier version's results on the same operation were seen (\cref{sec:leakage}).

\paragraph{Fitting variance.} Five seeds change some first-detection budgets by an order of magnitude (\cref{sec:variance}); the autoencoders and Euler were fitted once, so their position may move as much. Five draws also understate the spread of a sixth.

\paragraph{Units, ties, days and budgets.} Unit rankings carry no tie bracket and units are priced like events; only M2a and M0b have event-level brackets of non-zero width. The harmonised first test day is partial (\cref{sec:intrusion}). The staffing model behind the budgets rests on weak inputs, and the fixed-threshold variant shows that a top-$k$ budget is an idealisation.

\paragraph{Features.} For a user's first event, the deviation from the user's typical hour is set to zero, the most typical value. The temporal training partition begins at day 0 without history, while validation and test have a warm-up.

\paragraph{Campaign definition and rule mapping.} Campaign recall depends on the grouping threshold (\cref{sec:ties-results}) and counts each compromised account of the operation as a campaign. The ATT\&CK techniques attached to the rules (\cref{app:rules}) express design intent and are not validated against ground truth.

\paragraph{External validity.} One enterprise network observed in 2015, before widespread multi-factor authentication; labels are a partial ground truth; the red team did not attempt to evade these detectors; and the raw files come from a mirror whose content was verified on three of 58 days and in total.

\section{Scope of the claims}
\label{sec:scope}
For these detectors, universes and protocols, on one network and one operation: no campaign is detected within 10 events per day and at most a tenth within 500; at 100 events per day one detector notices the intrusion on its first, partial day; ROC-AUC tracks where most campaigns are found but did not indicate which detector finds the first; and in user-days a graph detector and a source-novelty score reach about half of the campaigns at 500 per day, against a few percent for a random ranking. The results do not show that ROC-AUC fails to predict budgeted detection in general, that any detector family is better than another beyond this operation, or that Euler's published average precision is wrong: they show that its released code and preprocessing, in the released environment, do not reach it on this data.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:reproducibility}
The code, configuration and run outputs are public at \url{https://github.com/SaniAdamou14/AuthBench} \parencite{authbenchsoftware}; the first version of this report is archived \parencite{authbenchpreprint}. Continuous integration runs linting, strict type checking and the test suite on every change, regenerates the synthetic results byte for byte, and regenerates every number and table of this report from the committed run outputs, failing if one differs; the generator also checks the run against itself and against the published counts it quotes. The evaluations were produced by the code at commit \texttt{bd833eb}, the Euler fidelity configurations by commit \texttt{6c12582}, and the authors' code at its commit \texttt{b6ab8fd}, on a rented machine with 16 virtual CPUs and 61\,GB of memory, in \RunHours{} hours from start to the last evaluation and about 17 hours of machine time in all; the authors' code needed about 36\,GiB. Outputs, logs, the authors' Euler results and every recount quoted in \cref{sec:euler} are committed under \texttt{reports/runs/20261022T102638Z}, but the Euler fidelity runs and the authors' code were driven by separate scripts with machine-specific paths and are not yet one command. Fitting and resampling are seeded, except in the authors' code.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Under a leak-free protocol and at the event level, none of thirteen detectors detects a campaign of the LANL red team within 10 alerts per day. Read at the level of the intrusion, one detector notices it on its first day at 100 alerts per day, and two others a week later at 50. ROC-AUC tracks where most campaigns are found but not which detector finds the first; that first detection depends on the fitting seed and on how the test days are cut, and a threshold calibrated on 41 hours does not hold for eight weeks. In user-days, a graph detector and a source-novelty score reach half of the campaigns at 500 per day. The authors' Euler code, with its released preprocessing, does not reach its published average precision. Because the labelled activity is one operation, these findings describe one case; the harness is meant to make the next case, on a second dataset and with the detectors this catalogue lacks, cheap to evaluate.

\section*{Ethics and data statement}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Ethics and data statement}
The LANL data are public and de-identified, and their use requires citation of the provider; the benchmark manipulates no personal data. It is defensive in purpose and contains no offensive technique. The authors' Euler code carries no licence; it was run unmodified apart from paths, and only those path edits are published with the run outputs. We have not yet contacted Euler's authors about the reproduction; their answer will be reported.
